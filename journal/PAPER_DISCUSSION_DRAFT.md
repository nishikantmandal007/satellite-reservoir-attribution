## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Climate Signal and the Residual Identification Problem

The central challenge in attributing reservoir storage variability to anthropogenic operations is the decomposition problem: a physically managed system simultaneously responds to climate forcing and to human decisions, and these two signals overlap in time and frequency. The XGBoost framework developed here frames this as a residual identification problem rather than a causal decomposition problem. A climate-only prediction model is first trained on non-collinear drivers representing the physically plausible meteorological state of the Damodar catchment; the unexplained residual then becomes the estimator of managed storage departure. This approach is philosophically aligned with the partial attribution methods used by Ji et al. (2023) and Wada et al. (2017) at global scales, but is applied here at reservoir-management resolution with operationally interpretable SHAP attribution.

A key finding that bears emphasis is the near-zero predictive skill of climate features alone: NSE = 0.018 when lag features are excluded. This is not a failure of the model; it is the finding. Damodar basin climate is insufficient to explain Maithon storage variability on a month-to-month basis. This result — that climate accounts for only a small fraction of storage variance in a managed reservoir — directly supports the broader thesis that basin-scale GRACE analyses, which attribute all TWS change to climate-driven groundwater and soil moisture flux, are systematically misleading for heavily managed systems. The anthropogenic fraction is not a correction term; in the Damodar system, it is the dominant source of sub-annual storage variability.

The dominance of the lagged storage anomaly (dS_lag1, mean |SHAP| = 0.064 BCM) in the attribution model requires careful interpretation. This feature captures reservoir operational inertia — the tendency for storage state to persist from one month to the next — which is partially a consequence of management decisions (how much water was released last month) and partially a consequence of hydrological memory (catchment wetness). The near-zero no-lag NSE (0.018) shows that once this lag term is removed, climate alone explains almost nothing, which means that the full model's NSE of 0.572 is largely carried by the operational memory term. We interpret this not as evidence that the model is misspecified, but as further confirmation that DVC operations dominate Maithon storage dynamics: the reservoir is managed to a multi-month target, and its current state is more informative about its future state than the prevailing climate.

### 5.2 Causal Independence and the Attribution Integrity Check

A persistent vulnerability in residual-based attribution is the possibility that the residual captures model underfit rather than a physically distinct signal. A model that is poorly specified or underpowered will produce large residuals regardless of whether an anthropogenic signal exists. The causal inference tests in Stage 4 directly address this vulnerability.

The Granger causality tests on STL remainders found no significant linear causality between Maithon and Durgapur storage anomalies (minimum p = 0.44). More importantly, the two null checks showed no evidence that ΔS_anthro causes ΔS_climate (p = 0.41) or that ΔS_climate causes ΔS_anthro (p = 0.57). Linear Granger non-significance is expected given the monthly temporal resolution of the dataset (n = 48 effective rows after STL decomposition; ideal sample size ≥ 100) and the fact that DVC flood control releases operate over 48–72 hours — a timescale that monthly aggregation cannot resolve. Granger non-significance should not be interpreted as evidence of causal independence; it is a reflection of the test's power at this sample size.

The convergent cross-mapping tests are more informative. CCM with 999 phase-randomised surrogates confirmed decisively that ΔS_anthro and ΔS_climate are causally uncoupled in both directions (ρ = 0.044 vs surr_95th = 0.371 for anthro-xmap-climate; ρ = 0.010 vs surr_95th = 0.410 for climate-xmap-anthro). The phase-randomised surrogate method, following Theiler et al. (1992), preserves the spectral structure of each series while destroying the cross-map structure, providing a null distribution that accounts for shared seasonality. The decisive non-significance of both null checks — ρ values are 6–40 times below the surrogate threshold — confirms that the XGBoost residual is not recovering a disguised climate signal through nonlinear interactions. This is the strongest available evidence that ΔS_anthro represents a genuinely distinct physical process.

The significant Maithon-to-Durgapur CCM (ρ = 0.294 > surr_95th = 0.144; convergence slope = +0.0042) warrants separate discussion. This cross-mapping reflects downstream hydraulic connectivity through the Damodar river channel: storage drawdowns at Maithon propagate as inflow pulses to Durgapur barrage over monthly timescales. The asymmetric direction (Maithon causes Durgapur, not vice versa) is physically consistent with the cascade geometry — Maithon is approximately 80 km upstream of Durgapur. This finding does not, by itself, prove coordinated operational scheduling, since it could arise from purely hydrological channel routing without any management involvement. However, taken together with the HMM regime analysis identifying DRAWDOWN months (mean ΔS_anthro = −0.168 BCM, 25% of months) and FILL months (+0.033 BCM, 75%), the pattern is consistent with managed cascade operations in which Maithon drawdowns signal elevated downstream inflow availability, creating storage opportunities at Durgapur.

### 5.3 GRACE-FO Reconciliation and the Cross-Scale Evidence

Claim C3 of this study — that reservoir-scale anthropogenic storage changes are detectable within GRACE-FO TWS anomalies after controlling for climate — is supported but requires careful qualification. The partial regression of GRACE-FO terrestrial water storage on ΔS_climate (ERA5-based) and ΔS_anthro yielded a statistically significant coefficient for the anthropogenic term (β₂ = +3.84 cm/BCM, HAC-corrected p < 0.001, 95% CI [+2.13, +5.55] cm/BCM). A physical sanity check confirms the magnitude is credible: the Damodar basin covers approximately 23,370 km², which corresponds to a theoretical GRACE sensitivity of 4.28 cm/BCM; the fitted coefficient is 90% of this theoretical value, well within the stated ±15–25% AV curve uncertainty.

However, the interpretation of this result requires restraint. GRACE measures all terrestrial water storage changes within its ~90,000 km² mascon cell, which substantially exceeds the Damodar basin. The ΔS_anthro signal from two reservoirs (Maithon and Durgapur, with combined live capacity ~1.39 BCM) represents a small fraction of the total mascon signal, which integrates groundwater, soil moisture, snow, and surface water across multiple basins. The partial regression design controls for the ERA5-based climate term, but cannot fully account for anthropogenic groundwater depletion from agricultural pumping in the broader Gangetic plain, which is the dominant GRACE-detected signal in this region (Rodell et al., 2009). We therefore frame the finding cautiously: the ΔS_anthro coefficient is of the correct sign, magnitude within theoretical expectation, and statistically significant (p < 0.001 after HAC correction for residual autocorrelation DW = 1.22). This is consistent with a contribution of DVC reservoir operations to GRACE-FO TWS anomalies, but not sufficient to uniquely attribute it. The GRACE-FO reconciliation should be read as supporting evidence for the broader claim that managed-reservoir operations contaminate basin-scale satellite water assessments, rather than as a closed-loop quantification of that contamination.

Interestingly, the partial R² of 0.187 for ΔS_anthro exceeds that for ΔS_climate in the regression. This reinforces the Stage 3 finding: in the Damodar mascon, the anthropogenic storage signal contributes as much to GRACE-detectable variability as the climate-forced component, at least in years when DVC operations produce large anomalies.

### 5.4 Operational Fingerprint Taxonomy and its Implications for Satellite Monitoring

The DTW-based operational fingerprint taxonomy reveals a level of operational consistency that was not anticipated a priori. Durgapur Barrage shows near-identical annual storage cycle morphology across all seven sample years (2018–2024), expressed as a single cluster at K = 3. This remarkable repeatability reflects the rigidity of the Rabi irrigation season schedule — defined by wheat sowing windows in West Bengal and Jharkhand — rather than any climate sensitivity. Because the barrage serves a fixed command area through a 2,494 km canal network with limited flexibility in release timing, its operational fingerprint is effectively a calendar event rather than a climate-responsive management decision.

Maithon shows more heterogeneity. The 2018 annual cycle occupies a distinct cluster characterised by exceptionally high peak storage (0.883 BCM, the dataset maximum), while the 2019–2024 cycles form a single lower-storage group. Whether this reflects a genuine secular trend in DVC storage policy, declining catchment inflows, or the statistical artefact of a small sample (n = 7 annual cycles) cannot be determined from the available data. The tentative interpretation — declining Maithon fill fraction from 2018 onward, potentially indicating increasing anthropogenic demand pressure or reduced monsoon inflow — warrants investigation with longer records when additional S1 data accumulates.

The practical implication of the taxonomy is significant. The fingerprints are constructed entirely from satellite-derived water area data, requiring no in-situ sensors, no reservoir operator cooperation, and no discharge gauge records. The annual cycles are discriminable with silhouette scores of 0.49 (all reservoirs, K = 3) and 0.44 (Maithon only, K = 2), both above the minimum threshold of 0.40 for meaningful cluster separation. This demonstrates that managed-reservoir operational patterns are learnable from passive satellite observations alone, opening a pathway to basin-scale operational monitoring for ungauged or data-sparse reservoir systems across South Asia and elsewhere.

The failure of Panchet to contribute to the taxonomy is a limitation worth noting. As the intermediate storage reservoir in the DVC cascade, Panchet likely captures the richest operational dynamics — coordinated pre-monsoon drawdown before Maithon begins filling, and emergency tandem flood releases. The exclusion of Panchet therefore likely underestimates the complexity of DVC operational fingerprints and represents the most significant gap in the current dataset. Re-running the GEE extraction with the corrected bounding box would add approximately 94 months of data and potentially increase taxonomy discriminability substantially.

### 5.5 Limitations and Uncertainties

Several limitations constrain the scope and confidence of the findings. First, the analysis is restricted to the 2017–2024 Sentinel-1 era (82 months), because MODIS data (2002–2013) cannot be cross-calibrated to SAR due to a four-year sensor gap. This precludes attribution analysis for the 2009 catastrophic flood — arguably the most operationally significant DVC event in the record — and limits characterisation of pre-2017 regime shifts. Second, the absolute magnitudes of ΔS_anthro carry a ±15–25% systematic uncertainty inherited from the DEM-based AV curve; relative patterns and anomaly directions are much more reliable. Third, the water balance is not closed: without inflow gauge data, it is not possible to partition ΔS_anthro into its constituent drivers (flood releases, irrigation releases, hydropower operations, or evaporation anomalies). The SCS-CN proxy captures bulk runoff but cannot distinguish management-driven from climate-driven inflow variation. Fourth, the Granger test is severely underpowered at n = 48, and its non-significance should not be interpreted as a finding of causal independence.

Despite these limitations, the convergence of evidence across five independent methods — XGBoost residual isolation, bootstrap uncertainty quantification, lag-robustness tests, CCM surrogate validation, and GRACE-FO partial regression — provides a robust multi-line case for the existence and detectability of anthropogenic fingerprints in DVC reservoir storage. Each method has its own failure modes and assumptions; the fact that all five yield consistent conclusions substantially reduces the probability that any single analysis artefact drives the overall result.

### 5.6 Broader Context and Future Directions

This study joins a growing body of work demonstrating that the attribution of GRACE TWS anomalies to groundwater depletion or climate change is confounded by reservoir and irrigation operations in heavily managed basins (Wada et al., 2017; Ji et al., 2023). The DVC case adds specificity to this general claim: using a single, intensively managed reservoir system with known operational mandates and documented historical events, it demonstrates not only that the anthropogenic signal exists but that it is nonlinearly coupled between reservoirs, expressible in a repeatable operational fingerprint, and consistent with the expected GRACE-FO sensitivity.

The operational fingerprint approach has particular relevance for Indian water management. India's GRACE-based groundwater depletion assessments (Rodell et al., 2009; Long et al., 2020) cover large administrative regions that include hundreds of small and medium reservoirs similar in scale to Maithon and Durgapur. If a significant fraction of GRACE-detected storage decline in these regions is attributable to reservoir operations — pre-monsoon drawdowns for flood preparedness, seasonal irrigation releases — rather than aquifer depletion, current policy responses (crop pattern diversification, micro-irrigation incentives) may be targeting the wrong driver. The framework developed here, applied at scale using freely available Sentinel-1 and GRACE-FO data, could provide a rapid national audit of reservoir-driven GRACE contamination within existing satellite data archives.

Future work should address three primary gaps: (i) re-analysis after inclusion of Panchet, which would complete the three-reservoir cascade and allow direct testing of tandem operational coordination; (ii) extension to the 2002–2013 MODIS epoch, potentially through cross-calibration with contemporary S1 scenes from comparable monsoon years; and (iii) application of the fingerprint taxonomy to a representative sample of DVC-analogous systems across the Indian peninsula, to test whether the four-archetype operational structure generalises beyond the Damodar case.
